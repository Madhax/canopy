## The question

The product's core claim is *the chart is the system*. Every chart is therefore a **hypothesis**: that this structure, these roles, these models, at these salaries, is a good way to discharge the purpose. Today the operator can draw the hypothesis, actuate it, and watch it burn tokens — but nothing in the product can tell them whether a *different* chart would have done the same work better, cheaper, or with less of their attention. The only comparison machinery in the corpus is PF-1's benchmark twin (`target-app.md` §10): one org against one bare session, run as an event, by hand, once.

Three questions, precisely:

1. **Comparison.** Given two teams and one task, decide which performed better — with a judgment the operator can inspect, trust, and overrule.
2. **The rubric.** Which factors count, at what weights; who judges the un-measurable ones; and how engineers change the factors without a code deploy.
3. **The search.** Make comparison iterative: something generates the next challenger (structure *and* model choices), trials run continuously, the human governs mostly retroactively — and after 100 iterations the whole history is still legible and steerable.

## The answer, in one paragraph

An **Experiment** is a standing comparison owned by an Organization: a purpose, a pool of **Tasks** (curated by the operator, replayed from the org's own closed intents, or generated-and-reviewed), a roster of **Variants** — frozen team blueprints (chart + profile bindings + salaries + schedule knobs), each recording its parent variant and the single **mutation** that produced it — and a versioned **Rubric**. A **Trial** takes one task and dispatches it to each enrolled variant as an ordinary Intent into an isolated, ephemeral variant Team: everything downstream — assignments, gates, plans, steps, meters — is stock machinery, which is what makes the results trustworthy. **The experiment layer adds no new work semantics; it composes intents, teams, and the ledger, and the only genuinely new records are the rubric, the verdicts, and the variant lineage.** Scoring separates instruments from opinions: *measured* factors (cost, rework, operator load, active time) come from the ledger and engine records and are never judged; *programmatic* factors (correctness, where a test suite or checkable contract exists) come from probe executors; *judged* factors come from a blinded evaluator panel comparing anonymized exhibits pairwise; *human* verdicts outrank everything and are recorded, never averaged away. A **search policy** proposes the next challenger — mechanical sweeps first (the model-downgrade sweep is the flagship), a proposer agent later — inside an operator-set **envelope**, with promotion of a champion into a real production team always a governed action behind an ApprovalGate. A leaderboard, a lineage tree, and per-trial comparison pages make a hundred iterations reviewable after the fact; every displayed score wears its rubric version, its evidence tier, and its sample size.

## The worked example, carried end to end

Organization `canopy-inc` opens experiment `ex-maint` — *"find the cheapest maintenance team that closes bugs no worse than today's."* The champion, variant **A**, is the `canopy-maintenance` blueprint as it runs in production: `engineering-lead`, `support-engineer` (triager), `backend-engineer`, `qa-engineer`, all on the default Fable profile. Challenger **B1**: same chart, `backend-engineer` rebound to a cheaper model tier. Challenger **B2**: the triager removed. The **solo baseline** — one `backend-engineer`, no managers — is enrolled automatically (PF-1, made continuous). The task pool: twelve bugs replayed from the team's own closed-intent history, eight more generated by the task-author and operator-reviewed; five of the twenty marked **holdout**. Rubric v1: correctness 40 (programmatic — the regression-suite probe), fix quality 20 (panel of three, blind pairwise), cost 20 (cache-aware estimated USD), operator load 10, rework 5, active time 5; guardrails: no recursion-boundary violation, deliverable matches contract, completes without an unresolved hard-stop.

Twelve trials later the leaderboard reads: **B1** holds the correctness floor, costs −38% against the champion, and wins 7 of 9 scored trials on composite. The promotion predicate fires; B1 confirms on the five holdout tasks it has never influenced; a `champion-suggested` card lands in the inbox; the operator ratifies the ApprovalGate and the blueprint graduates onto the production team — the receipt in the feed reads *"B1 promoted: −38% cost, quality held, 12 trials · rubric v1."* **B2** breaches the correctness floor twice (fixes shipped without reproduction) and is retired; the lineage records exactly why. The solo baseline wins the `trivial`-tagged slice outright and loses the `gnarly` slice — which is PF-1's answer, published continuously, whichever way it falls.

## The four pillars

- **Same machinery, new question.** A trial is ordinary intents into ordinary teams. No parallel execution path, no special agent mode — if the experiment layer needed its own work semantics, its results would say nothing about production.
- **Instruments before opinions.** Anything the platform already records is *measured*, never judged: cost from the ledger (cache-aware, per F1), rework from the state machine, operator load from gate ownership, time from step records. Judgment is reserved for what instruments cannot reach — and every displayed number carries its source tier, its rubric version, and its sample size (the capacity console's honesty rules, applied to scores).
- **The lab has no authority.** Task authors, judges, and proposers only ever produce artifacts; the platform records; the operator ratifies. Governance is deliberately *retroactive* inside a pre-approved envelope — review the lineage when you choose to — and prospective at exactly two doors: instantiating outside the envelope, and promoting a champion into production.
- **Goodhart walls.** The rubric is a target, so information flows are walled by construction: variants never see the rubric; the proposer never sees holdout tasks; judges never see team identities or costs; humans audit the judges. (`02` §7 is the full wall table.)

## Scope

**In scope:** the experiment domain (experiments, variants, trials, tasks, rubrics, verdicts); measured/programmatic/judged/human scoring with tiers; blinded pairwise panel judging with human audit and override; task curation, replay, and reviewed generation with holdout discipline; mechanical variant generators (model-downgrade and structure sweeps) and the proposer role; champion–challenger operation, promotion as a governed action; the lab UX surfaces; continuous cadence-driven operation under capacity governance.

**Non-goals (this series):** production traffic splitting — trials replay or generate tasks, they never intercept a live intent (shadow-mirroring live intents is a possible future mode, noted in `01` §9); bandit/Bayesian auto-tuning beyond champion–challenger; cross-experiment meta-learning; model fine-tuning (this searches *structure* space, not weight space); crowd or external evaluation; statistical machinery beyond paired win rates, floors, and minimum sample sizes (no p-value theater over n=9); routing production work across specialized champions (a real idea the per-tag leaderboard will provoke; deliberately a separate future design — `03` §8).

## Relationship to the existing corpus

This series **generalizes** PF-1's benchmark twin into standing machinery (the solo baseline is a first-class, default-on variant; `target-app.md` §10 becomes "Experiment #1"). It **staffs** `org-roadmap.md` O5: the roles forge's QA bar — *"a candidate role revision must beat the incumbent on the eval suite before the lead proposes it"* — is exactly a rubric-scored experiment, so O5's eval harness is this series' API rather than a bespoke tool. It **is** `testing.md`'s post-MVP row ("LLM-judged role-content evals — the one genuinely new kind of testing"), built as product surface rather than CI. It gives `domain-model.md` §Calibration its active form: calibration fits envelopes from what teams *did*; experiments search what teams *could be*. It respects `manager-responsibilities.md`'s deliberate ⛔ — no org restructures itself — by routing all structural change through artifacts, platform instantiation, and operator ratification (`03` §7). And it inherits the capacity series' posture wholesale: experiments are `batch` priority, runway-courteous, and legible on the portfolio surfaces (`05` §5).
